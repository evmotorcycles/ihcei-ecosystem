One word behaves that way. The other doesn't.

The claim was that yahūd and naṣārā are not labels but descriptions of something done. That prediction is checkable, so it was checked against the whole text. It holds for one word and fails for the other — and the failure is the more interesting half.

Named by a verb
ٱلَّذِينَ هَادُوا۟
alladhīna hādū — "those who hādū"
10 times the group is named by a finite verb — including a direct vocative, "O you who hādū" (62:6). The noun ٱلْيَهُود appears only 8 times. The verb is commoner than the noun.
Named by a noun, only
ٱلنَّصَـٰرَىٰ
al-naṣārā
0 times the group is named by a finite verb — despite the noun appearing 14 times and the same root supplying 82 finite verbs elsewhere in the text.

Why "it shares a root with a verb" proves nothing

The obvious test is worthless, and this text hands you the counterexample.

عَاد
ʿĀd — 26 ayahs

The tribe. Surface-identical to two entirely unrelated words: عَادَ ʿāda, "he returned" (root ʿ-W-D, 2:275 wa man ʿāda), and عَاد ʿādin, "transgressor" (root ʿ-D-W, 2:173 wa lā ʿād).

A test based on shared letters would confidently announce that a proper noun is deverbal. So that test was not used.

The test that was used instead

Does the text designate the group with a finite verb — a relative clause "those who [verb]" or a vocative "O you who [verb]" — rather than only with a noun?

A proper noun cannot be conjugated to name its own bearers. If a text says "those who X-ed" as a standing way of naming a group, the designation is built on the act. That's a behaviour, and behaviours can be counted.

The gate that failed, and why it's the best part

The prediction was that naṣārā is never designated by a verb. The matcher found two hits.

وَٱلَّذِينَ ءَاوَوا۟ وَّنَصَرُوٓا۟
8:72 · 8:74 — "and those who sheltered and helped"

The construction does exist for this root. But it names the Anṣār — the helpers — who are a different group from the naṣārā.

The matcher cannot resolve who a phrase refers to, so it counted them. The gate is scored FAILED as measured — a gate is not re-scored after the fact because you found a reason you dislike the answer.

But the substance is sharper than a pass would have been:

The root can name a group by what it did. It just never does that for the naṣārā.

ن‑ص‑ر supplies 82 finite verbs and the noun anṣār ("helpers") 11 times. The machinery for a "those who did X" designation is fully present in the text and is actually used — for the Anṣār.

The naṣārā are named by a noun 14 times and by a verb 0 times. That is not an absence of opportunity. It's a pattern.

So a single claim covering both words is not supported. Whatever is true of one is not thereby true of the other, and stating them together would hide the asymmetry that is the actual result.

What the run does not do

It does not establish what the words mean

Word behaviour constrains readings. It does not select one. Many perfectly ordinary ethnonyms are historically deverbal in every language — that's how a great many group-names are formed. So a pass here is consistent with a descriptor reading and does not establish it.

That limit is gate V8, scored as untestable-here rather than buried in a footnote.

It is not a claim about any living community

The units of analysis are Arabic word-forms in one text. No gate references any real-world group, and nothing measured here is evidence about Jewish or Christian people, beliefs or practices. That is gate V9, out of scope by construction.

Worth saying plainly in both directions: a finding that a word is built on an action rather than on descent is, if anything, the opposite of an ethnic claim.

Where the real difficulty was

Not the statistics. The spelling.

The dataset writes long ā with a dagger alif (U+0670): نَصَـٰرَىٰ. Strip it as a diacritic and the word becomes نصري, which matches nothing. It has to become a full alif.

But a dagger alif following alif-maqsura is that letter's own vowel, not an extra letter — handle it second and naṣārā grows a spurious alif.

And marks must be removed by Unicode category, not a hand-written range. A hand-written range missed U+064B–U+0652 — fatha, damma, shadda, sukun — and silently corrupted every count in the first attempt.

The run reproduces the published benchmark counts — naṣārā 14, al-yahūd 8, hādū 10 — which is an integrity check, not a finding.

One weakness, admitted

The primary gate's threshold (≥ 5) was set after the count of 10 had been seen during that normalisation work. So the primary is a weak pre-registration and is labelled that way. The control and the split were not informed that way, and they are what carry the run.
