The schedules are clean. They still can't settle it.

All three contracts are cash-flow identical to plain debt as recorded — but that does not make them debt. It means the data contains no moment at which the difference could show, and that is a fact about the dataset, not the contracts.

The questionNot "do they perform well" — can they audit at all?

A dataset can only test a claim it is capable of discriminating. The claim separating these contracts from conventional debt is that the financier bears asset risk rather than holding a fixed claim. That has exactly one observable consequence:

When the asset loses value, the financier's position must fall with it.

Declared before the files were analysed

N = 5, 5 and 10. These are contract schedules, not outcome records. No statistical inference is licensed and none is made — no p-value, no interval, no generalisation. A test asserts the results file contains no statistical claim.

The standard critique says cost-plus is interest wearing a different hat. A disguised loan would hold the annualised rate fixed and vary the flat markup. This does the opposite — a flat 10% margin regardless of tenor, so the implied annual rate swings fourfold.

That is what a price that does not price time looks like. Recorded because it ran against the direction expected.

The bank holds legal title in all five periods while the lessee pays every maintenance charge — 3,000 USD billed straight into the lease payment. Ownership cost transferred to the non-owner; ownership risk stayed nominal.

Reading itStated carefully in both directions

What it does not show

It does NOT show these contracts are debt. A6 shows they genuinely differ the instant something goes wrong. Anyone quoting A5 as proof of "disguised debt" would be misusing it — and the pre-registration forbids that reading in advance, just as it forbids rescuing the result by appeal to principle.

What it does show — the oxymoron charge, made precise

The charge lands because labels are unverifiable. Here we can say exactly why verification fails: a planned schedule with no adverse event is structurally incapable of exhibiting risk-sharing, however the contract is drafted.

Publishing schedules can never answer the charge. Only outcome records can.

RemedyWhat would make the model auditable

A different dataset, not a different contract. It must contain at least one event at which a risk-bearer and a fixed claimant would behave differently.

NeededWhy
Asset values marked over timethe co-owner's position must be observed moving
Arrears and missed paymentsa fixed claim accrues; a participation claim absorbs
Write-downs actually takenthe single most discriminating record there is
Early settlementsreveals whether profit was earned or unwound pro-rata
Defaults and recovery outcomesshows who ended up bearing the loss — the whole question
Maintenance and repair incidencedistinguishes an owner from a titleholder

One quarter of real portfolio data containing a single write-down would be worth more than a thousand rows of clean schedule.

A schedule with no adverse event carries zero discriminating information no matter how large it gets.

UpstreamWhat this changes, including in our own code

The three-proposal benchmark modelled this position from a written description because no data existed. That remains the honest status — these schedules do not replace it, because they cannot adjudicate the property being modelled.

What they do establish, and it is not nothing:

  • the schedules are arithmetically clean, so the modelled mechanics were fair
  • the cost-plus markup does not price time — supports modelling it as a trade margin, not interest
  • the co-ownership rental is a constant rate on the outstanding stake — modelling it as a declining-balance payment was correct
  • the lease bills maintenance to the lessee, which our model does not reflect
A gap in our own implementation, found by this data

That last point is recorded here rather than quietly patched. Changing the engine after seeing an audit result is exactly what pre-registration exists to prevent. It belongs in the next spec.
