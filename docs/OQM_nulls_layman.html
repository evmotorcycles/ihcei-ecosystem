What our failures taught us

Most of our tests did not work. That is the good news — and this page explains, without jargon, exactly how each failure made the Five Governance Questions smaller, clearer and harder to fake.

Start with the master key

Imagine claiming you have a master key that opens every door in a large building. You could say it forever and never be wrong — until you actually walk the corridors and try it. Then you find some doors take keycards, some have combination dials, and some have no lock at all.

We walked the corridors. We had one equation and we tried it on protein networks, software projects, banks, package repositories and quantum physics. It opened some doors and jammed in others.

Every jam taught us something a success could not. A key that opens everything tells you nothing about the locks. A key that fails on door four tells you exactly what kind of door four is.

Three habits the failures forced on us

Habit one
Surface appearance is not the mechanism
Checking a car's paint tells you nothing about the engine. Counting how often a number repeats in a spreadsheet told us nothing about whether information can actually get through a network. Every cheap shortcut we tried, failed.
Habit two
Different shapes need different tools
In a bucket brigade, everyone must catch and pass — both are bottlenecks. In a radio broadcast, passing it on is free because thousands already received it. Same information, completely different mathematics.
Habit three
Write down the answer before you look
Every test was locked with a cryptographic seal before the data was touched. When a test failed we recorded the failure. We never went back and added an excuse.

The Five Questions, sharpened

Here is where each question stands now — and, just as importantly, how much of it we have actually tested.

Question 1 · Purpose
A full tank won't move a car with a broken gearbox.
Talent, money and resources on their own are inert. Value appears only when capacity is coupled to careful work. Tested and holding on 4,825 proteins and 992 software projects.
Question 2 · Realms
Distance may be a kind of waiting.
The idea that physical distance is really information delay is written down as a testable proposal with the exact laboratory experiments named. It has not been tested in a lab. No result here supports it yet.
Question 3 · Stewardship
In a relay, you must catch the baton and pass it.
Receiving well and giving on are both required, and hoarding your output collapses the whole result to nothing. But only on the right shape of network — that is the narrowing, and it cost us a 195× error to learn.
Question 4 · Reference-lock
You cannot navigate with two different Norths.
Measuring against several competing standards at once makes the measurement meaningless. We have practised this, not proved it — our own discipline working is not evidence that the idea is true of the world.
Question 5 · Predictability
You can't hear an engine failing from a photograph.
Systems bleed slowly rather than snapping. So health has to be watched over time — how fast a group closes its own open problems. The strongest single result we have, and it survived a check for hidden size effects.
The honest total
One of five has been put at real risk and it lost.
Question 3 was tested in quantum physics and the two-hop form was beaten badly. Questions 2 and 4 have no supporting test at all yet. Saying so is the point. A framework that reports its own empty shelves is the only kind worth trusting.

Three things in the earlier summary that the record does not support

Correcting these matters more than the wins, because an overstatement that goes unchallenged becomes the thing people quote.

“Distance is information lag” is not established

It is a written proposal with named experiments — butterfly-front scaling, a transfer test, a tunable-range boundary, and a mandatory hardware control. None has been run in a laboratory. It should be described as awaiting test, never as a finding.

“Two-hop stewardship is mandatory on interbank networks” overstates what we found

On the real 2016 interbank network the two forms came out effectively tied — 0.6090 against 0.6109, and the simpler rival was marginally ahead. A later run did favour the two-hop form there, but our own audit declared that run too small to judge, so it cannot be cited either way.

Question 4 was not “vindicated”

Our pre-registration discipline did hold. But our method working is not evidence for a claim about the world. Confusing the two would be exactly the error the discipline exists to prevent. Question 4 remains untested.

This is the most useful thing the whole exercise produced, and it protects OQM rather than limiting it.

The boundary, stated plainly

Reading a word as a function generates a hypothesis and a vocabulary. It never generates evidence. Root analysis appears in none of our calculations — a test literally scans the code to make sure of it.

And here is the case that proves the boundary is real: the method named a structure correctly and got the performance claim badly wrong. It produced the two-register design for our settlement work — and the measurement then found the routing rule on top of it was beaten by 18 of 20 random assignments. Right vocabulary. Wrong claim. Exactly the division of labour it should expect.

Why this is good for OQM

The most common charge against any interpretive method is that it can never be wrong. A framework that explains every outcome explains nothing. If a result confirms it, that's proof; if a result contradicts it, the scope is adjusted. Nothing can ever dislodge it — and so nobody outside has any reason to take it seriously.

This body of work is the answer to that charge, and the answer is made almost entirely of failures.

We published a 195× error against our own central equation. We recorded that our selector rule scored 1 of 4. We wrote down a prediction, got the direction right and the location wrong, and said so. We withdrew a sentence from one of our own pre-registrations when it turned out to over-claim. Each of those is a place where the framework could have been protected and wasn't.

That is what OQM gains. Not a bigger claim — a smaller one that can be checked. The method keeps what it is genuinely good at: producing an operational vocabulary and auditable readings. It stops borrowing credibility from numbers it did not earn. And because the empty shelves are labelled, anyone can walk up and try to fill one.

Nothing here settles any question of belief. These are measurements of networks, ledgers and equations. Two instruments can agree perfectly and still differ in what they mean — and what a thing means is not something a dataset was ever going to decide.
