What makes a channel measurable

Yeast cells and software projects have both been tested as two-hop communication channels, and both passed real checks. Here is what those checks actually were — and exactly what it would take to run the same checks on scripture, which nobody has done yet.

First, what a "channel" means here

Strip the equation away. The idea underneath is very simple.

What you're given
Capacity Your size, reach, resources. Handed to you. You didn't earn it.
Hop one — in
Taking it in well How cleanly you receive and sort what reaches you. Signal from noise.
Hop two — out
Passing it on well How cleanly it leaves you and reaches others. Hoarding counts as failure.
What comes out
Actual yield What the whole thing produced. Not what it claimed.

The three multiply. So if either hop goes to zero, the yield goes to zero — no matter how much capacity was there. A huge broadcaster who receives nothing useful produces nothing useful. A brilliant receiver who tells nobody produces nothing either.

That's the claim. It sounds obvious. The whole question is whether you can check it, and three specific checks are what separate a measured channel from a nice story.

Substrate one

Baker's yeast

4,825 proteins inside a living cell, and the physical contacts between them. 1,055 of those genes turn out to be essential.

Substrate two

Open-source software

992 public code projects, tracked over years, some still alive and some abandoned.

Substrate three

Scripture

Read as a channel: sincere seeking is hop one, selfless sharing is hop two. Not yet tested. This page explains what testing it would mean.

The three checks

Any claim of the form "this is a two-hop channel" has to survive all three. They are not statistics tricks. Each one is a question a sceptical friend would ask.

Check one

Are the two hops actually two things?

The sceptical question: "You say there are two hops. Are you sure you haven't just measured one thing twice and given it two names?"

If "takes it in well" and "passes it on well" always rise and fall together, they're probably the same underlying quality wearing two labels — and the two-hop story is decoration.

There's a standard way to check. You get a number where 1.0 means the two are completely independent, and bigger numbers mean they're increasingly the same thing. Above 5.0 and the claim is dead.

Yeast
1.0026

How tightly a protein knits with its neighbours, versus how much traffic routes through it globally. Almost perfectly separate. Passes.

Software
1.0203

How consistent a project's own work is, versus how well it absorbs outsiders' contributions. Also separate. Passes.

Scripture
not run

Nobody has measured whether sincere seeking and selfless sharing come apart in any real population. Askable. Never asked.

Check two

Is the scoreboard independent of the game?

The sceptical question: "Who decides whether it worked — and did they use the same information you used to make the prediction?"

This is the one that catches almost everybody, and it is the reason the first two substrates count for anything.

Yeast

Someone deletes that gene in a physical dish and watches whether the cell dies. That happens in a laboratory. It has nothing whatsoever to do with the network map used to make the prediction. Fully independent.

Software

Did the project get shut down or go silent for two years? That's recorded in lifecycle metadata, separately from any quality measure. Independent.

Scripture

This is the hard one, and pretending otherwise helps nobody. If the reading is judged by whether the reading feels right, the scoreboard is the game.

We fell into exactly this trap three days ago

A third dataset, software packages, was being quoted as evidence: a +0.5695 correlation between a project's size and its "fidelity." It looked like a real finding and it had been cited for months.

Then we opened the definition. "Fidelity" had been defined as 1 ÷ (1 + months since last release) — that is, pure recency. And "size" was the number of releases.

So the finding was: packages that have released more times have released more recently. Which is not a discovery. It is the same fact stated twice, and the two numbers turn out to be algebraically the same, not merely similar.

That result carried zero information about fidelity, and it had been sitting in our own evidence pile. Nobody spotted it until someone asked the boring question: what exactly is being measured, and could it have come out differently?

Check three

Does the two-hop shape beat a simpler shape?

The sceptical question: "Fine, it fits. Would something dumber have fit just as well?"

A theory that fits isn't worth much if a simpler one fits equally. So you run the rivals side by side on the same data and see which loses.

Yeast

Two-hop beat the squared alternative at predicting which genes are essential. Passes.

Software

The squared alternative was ruled out. Projects don't fall off a cliff — they bleed out slowly. Passes, in the weaker sense that a rival was eliminated.

Scripture

Has the two-hop reading ever been run against a one-hop reading on anything? No.

And it does not pass everywhere. On a real national banking network, the two-hop form scored 0.6090 and the simpler rival 0.6109 — the rival marginally ahead. A tie. That honest result is why this page says "two substrates," not "everywhere."

So where does scripture actually stand?

Reading scripture as an intact channel is a reading, not a measurement. That is not an insult — yeast and software weren't measurements either, until someone specified the three checks and ran them.

The difference is only this: for those two, the checks were written down before the data was looked at, and then run, and then published whether they passed or not.

Saying "scripture is an intact channel because the equation holds on yeast" doesn't follow. The equation holding on yeast is a fact about proteins. It transfers to nothing else without its own test — which is exactly why a third dataset was quietly wrong for months.

Nor does the opposite follow. Untested is not refuted. The reading may well be right. It simply hasn't been put anywhere it could have come out wrong.

What it would take, concretely

Not a metaphor. Five steps, the same five that yeast and software went through.

  1. Choose who is being measured. Individual readers? Study circles? Whole communities? Pick one and say how many.
  2. Define the two hops so they cannot be the same number. Then run check one. If seeking and sharing turn out to move together, the two-hop reading is one hop with two names, and that's worth knowing.
  3. Pick an outcome nobody in the study grades. The hardest step by far. Something that happens whether or not anyone approves of it — the equivalent of the cell living or dying in the dish. If you can't name one, say so plainly rather than substituting a judgement call.
  4. Write the thresholds down and lock them before looking at any data. What counts as a pass, what counts as a fail, decided in advance.
  5. Publish it if it fails. This is the step that makes the other four mean anything.

Step three is where this will stand or fall, and it deserves to be said clearly: it may turn out that no independent outcome exists for the claim as posed. That would be a real finding too — it would mean the reading is a way of organising thought rather than a hypothesis about the world, and there is nothing shameful in that, provided it is said.

Why the checks are worth trusting

Because they keep catching us, not other people.

In the past week these same three questions have refuted our own published domain limit, disqualified our own most-cited correlation, and shown that a monitoring signal we recommended loses to simply counting a project's stars.

Six consecutive experiments were voided by a data-quality floor we set ourselves, and the floor was not lowered to let them through.

These aren't hurdles being placed in front of scripture. They're the ones that just knocked down three of our own claims — which is the only reason they're worth applying to anything.
