A print shop, a flood, and who the bank protects.

We built a lending system with two settings and a clever way to pick between them. We tested the clever part against flipping a coin. The coin won 18 times out of 20, so we deleted it and shipped the rest.

The modelTwo lists, and which one you're on decides who gets protected

Every loan sits in one of two registers. A register is just a list. Which list your loan is on decides what happens when things go wrong.

Recovery register
  • A normal fixed claim
  • Bad month → you still owe it
  • Good year later → the lender gets paid
  • Protects the lender
Containment register
  • A share-in-the-outcome claim
  • Bad month → it gets written down
  • Good year later → that money is gone forever
  • Protects everyone downstream of you

Neither one is better. They protect different people. That is the whole model.

And running underneath both is the piece that turned out to matter most — and which appeared in nobody's proposal: you don't pay on the 1st of the month. You pay a share of every payment as it arrives.

Worked exampleSam's print shop

Read this first

This example is an illustration, not a measurement. The numbers are invented to show how the mechanism works. The actual evidence comes from 10,000 real recorded transactions and is further down. We keep the two strictly apart.

Sam runs a small print shop. He needs £20,000 for a new machine. The shop normally takes about £8,000 a month.

Step 1 · where the money comes from

Savings that already exist — not new money

Under a conventional loan the bank creates most of that £20,000 as a new deposit when it writes the loan. Here it comes from savers' money that already exists.

Why it matters: when we tested creating money against not creating it, creating it made settlement worse every single time — more leverage, more failed payments. That's a measurement, not a principle.

Step 2 · a normal week

Money in, share out, same hour

MondaySam invoices a customer £3,000
WednesdayThe customer pays
Wednesday, same hour£1,500 goes to the funder. £1,500 stays with Sam.

No monthly due date. No "did the direct debit clear?" The payment is a share of what came in.

Step 3 · a bad quarter

Sam's biggest client leaves. Revenue falls to £2,000.

Old way: Sam owes his fixed payment on the 1st regardless. He can't pay. He is now in default — credit damaged, penalties running — and he stops paying his paper supplier and his part-time staff to cover the bank.

This model: £2,000 comes in, so £1,000 goes to the funder. That's it. Sam paid exactly what he was supposed to pay. He is not in default. He was never late.

Most small businesses don't fail because they're unprofitable. They fail because a fixed payment date met a variable income.

Step 4 · a real loss

A flood destroys stock. £6,000 is genuinely gone.

If Sam is in RECOVERY
  • Sam still owes the £6,000; it stays on the books
  • Two years on, trading well, it gets paid
  • The funder gets almost everything back
  • But Sam carried that debt through his whole recovery — and his supplier felt it
If Sam is in CONTAINMENT
  • The £6,000 is written down. Gone.
  • Sam is clear immediately and keeps paying his supplier and staff
  • So they don't fail either
  • The funder never sees that £6,000 again — not even if Sam has a record year
Step 5 · who decides

Not an algorithm. We tried, and it lost to a coin.

Our risk model was beaten by 18 of 20 random assignments, because the signal it used turned out to point slightly the wrong way. So the register is a policy set in advance and applied uniformly — for example, "all trade credit in our food-distribution chain goes in containment; all equipment finance goes in recovery." Simple, cheap, and it beat our clever version.

Two things we got wrong, published anyway

Our own scoring formula was broken. We combined two goals at "equal 50/50 weights", but on this data one term was 90× larger than the other, so the formula really only measured one of them. We did not rewrite it afterwards — we reported the result as scored and flagged that three of the five gates are compromised by it.

We overstated a finding and corrected it. We first wrote that the routing lost to every random draw. It lost to 18 of 20; two were worse. The corrected claim is the one that stands.

For a bankWhat to use, what to skip, what we did not achieve

Use tomorrow — no new infrastructure
  • Pay-as-you-collect on distressed exposures. Biggest measured effect in the whole programme, and it is mostly a change to collection timing.
  • Full reserve in the settlement layer — not as ideology, as the configuration with the fewest failed settlements.
  • Write-down tranches as a circuit breaker, where one customer's failure would take out three others.
  • A tamper-evident claim register, so "which register was this loan in?" is provable rather than asserted.
Do not buy
  • A risk-routing model. Ours failed. Be sceptical of anyone selling one who hasn't tested it against random assignment at the same share.
  • Risk-sharing as a way to lose less money. It measurably loses you more.
What we did NOT achieve — the commercially relevant fact

We never beat a conventional central clearing book on routine friction. A single full-reserve central book posted zero failed settlements against our decentralised mesh's 2,548. We tested whether the mesh repays that cost during a crisis across eighteen scenarios. It never did.

So this is not a replacement for a clearing house, and anyone claiming otherwise is not reading our numbers. It is four specific, separately-testable practices — plus one honest warning about the practice everyone expects to be the star.

Both sectorsThe same pitch, with one correction each

Conventional banks

No cultural framing required. Pay as you collect, don't lend what you haven't got, use write-downs surgically where contagion is the real exposure, and don't buy a scoring model that hasn't been tested against chance.

Islamic banks

Full reserve is vindicated by measurement, not doctrine — it was simply the best-performing configuration tested, which is a stronger argument and needs nobody to share your premises.

But risk-sharing raises your losses, not lowers them — 94.4 against 16.1. Its genuine value is systemic. Sold as prudential infrastructure it is defensible; sold as a better return profile it is not.

The "oxymoron" charge lands because labels are unverifiable. A tamper-evident register makes the label checkable — that is the real differentiator, and it is available to both sectors equally.

LastWhy publish a failure

A model that only ever produces confirmations hasn't been tested. We designed this from earlier measurements, wrote down in advance what would prove the new idea wrong, ran it, watched the idea fail exactly as predicted, and shipped the system smaller than planned.

Nine times in a row now we've declined to write a simulation whose output would have been decided by its own formula.

What's left is what survived a real attempt to kill it. That is the only kind of finding worth putting in front of a risk committee.
